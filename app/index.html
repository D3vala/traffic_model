How far to trust this

  1. These are reported crashes. The data is MMDA's Twitter feed. A missing tweet is not a missing crash.
  2. Past trends are not projected. EDSA's reports fell over the study window, but the feed itself got quieter, and independent PSA data moved the other way. The simulation holds the trend at its end-of-window level.
  3. The traffic response is assumed. How much crashes rise with traffic can't be measured from this data, so the assumption selector tries three values.
  4. Eighteen months of data. September 2018 to February 2020. Later months are excluded because of the lockdown and two months with no records.
  5. Zones are approximate. Five stretches per road, cut from GPS points. They show where reports fell, not where a vehicle is most at risk.

How the simulation works
  1. Count only the 12,570 rows MMDA classified as crashes, on EDSA and C5, inside September 2018 – February 2020.
  2. Fit a Negative Binomial model — a count model that allows more spread than a plain average — to 36 corridor-months, with traffic volume as the exposure denominator and a control for how often the account posted.
  3. Hold the fitted trend frozen at the end of the window, because projecting an 18-month trend forward would be extrapolation the data does not support.
  4. Draw 1,000 simulated years per scenario, each carrying sampling noise, parameter uncertainty, and one of three elasticity assumptions — elasticity is how strongly crashes respond to traffic, swept at 0.8, 1.0, and 1.2 because the data cannot measure it.
  5. Place every simulated crash into a zone and an hour using the historical joint profile; the event layer and the count model agree within 1.6%. In held-out testing (Nov 2019 – Feb 2020) the typical error was about 16% for EDSA and 32% for C5.
